# Chain Rule

## 🧠 Big Idea

The chain rule differentiates a **composition** — a function inside another function. When one rate of change feeds into another, you multiply the rates together.

It's the most-used derivative rule, because most real functions are built by nesting simpler ones.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\,f(g(x))=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)
$$

This means: take the derivative of the **outer** function (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the **inner** function. You differentiate from the outside in, multiplying each layer's rate.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine gears linked together. If gear A spins twice as fast as gear B, and gear B spins three times as fast as gear C, then A spins $2 \times 3 = 6$ times as fast as C. Rates of change multiply through the chain.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Differentiate $h(x)=(3x+1)^4$. The outer function is $(\,\cdot\,)^4$, the inner is $3x+1$:

$$
h'(x)=4(3x+1)^3 \cdot \underbrace{3}_{g'(x)}=12(3x+1)^3
$$

## ⚠️ Careful

Don't forget the inner derivative $g'(x)$ — multiplying by it is the whole point. Writing just $4(3x+1)^3$ misses the factor of $3$.

## 🔗 Connection

In Leibniz notation the chain rule reads $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}$. The intermediate $du$ appears to cancel, which is a helpful way to remember that rates multiply through each layer.

## 🔑 Remember

Chain rule: derivative of the outside, times derivative of the inside.
