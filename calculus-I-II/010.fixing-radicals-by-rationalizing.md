# Fixing Radicals by Rationalizing

## 🧠 Big Idea

When a limit with a square root gives $\frac{0}{0}$, factoring usually won't help. Instead, multiply by the **conjugate** to clear the radical and expose the hidden cancellation.

This is the standard trick whenever a stubborn root is causing the trouble.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B} \;\xrightarrow{\;\times\frac{\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}}{\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}}\;}\; \frac{A-B}{\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B}}
$$

This means: multiplying a difference of roots by its conjugate uses $(\sqrt{A}-\sqrt{B})(\sqrt{A}+\sqrt{B})=A-B$. The square roots disappear from the spot causing $\frac{0}{0}$, and a cancelable factor appears.

## 👀 Intuition

The conjugate is a partner that "undoes" the root through the difference-of-squares pattern. By multiplying top and bottom by the same conjugate, you change the form without changing the value — then the troublesome factor cancels. Multiplying by $\frac{\text{conjugate}}{\text{conjugate}}$ is just multiplying by $1$, so the limit stays exactly the same.

## 🔍 Simple Example

$$
\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x+1}-1}{x}
$$

Multiply by the conjugate $\sqrt{x+1}+1$:

$$
\frac{(\sqrt{x+1}-1)(\sqrt{x+1}+1)}{x(\sqrt{x+1}+1)}=\frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+1}+1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+1}+1}
$$

Now substitute $x=0$:

$$
\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+1}+1}=\frac{1}{2}
$$

## ⚠️ Careful

Multiply by the conjugate of **whichever part contains the root**. If the root is in the denominator, rationalize the bottom instead. And don't expand the part that didn't change — leaving $\sqrt{x+1}+1$ as a factor is what lets the cancellation work.

## 🔑 Remember

A square root causing $\frac{0}{0}$? Multiply by the conjugate to set the cancellation free.
