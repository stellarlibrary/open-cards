# Trigonometric Substitution

## 🧠 Big Idea

Trigonometric substitution rewrites certain radical expressions using sine, tangent, or secant. It is especially useful when an integral contains patterns like $a^2-x^2$, $a^2+x^2$, or $x^2-a^2$.

The reason it works is geometric: these algebraic expressions match right-triangle identities. A radical that looks complicated in $x$ may become a simple trig expression in $\theta$.

This substitution is temporary. After integrating in $\theta$, you must translate the answer back into the original variable $x$, usually using the original substitution or a right triangle.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\sqrt{a^2-x^2}: x=a\sin\theta, \qquad
\sqrt{a^2+x^2}: x=a\tan\theta, \qquad
\sqrt{x^2-a^2}: x=a\sec\theta
$$

The first substitution uses $1-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta$. The second uses $1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta$. The third uses $\sec^2\theta-1=\tan^2\theta$. Each choice is designed to simplify the radical.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine replacing an algebra problem with a right triangle. For $x=a\sin\theta$, the triangle has opposite side $x$ and hypotenuse $a$, so the remaining side is $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$. That triangle helps you convert back from $\theta$ to $x$ at the end.

The triangle also checks the algebra: each side length should match the radical pattern you started with.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Evaluate

$$
\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{9-x^2}}
$$

Since $9-x^2$ has the form $a^2-x^2$, let $x=3\sin\theta$. Then $dx=3\cos\theta\,d\theta$ and, on the usual range where $\cos\theta\ge 0$, $\sqrt{9-x^2}=3\cos\theta$. Thus

$$
\int \frac{3\cos\theta\,d\theta}{3\cos\theta}=\int d\theta=\theta+C
$$

Because $x=3\sin\theta$, we have $\theta=\arcsin(x/3)$. The answer is $\arcsin(x/3)+C$.

## 🔑 Remember

Choose the trig substitution whose identity matches the radical’s shape.
