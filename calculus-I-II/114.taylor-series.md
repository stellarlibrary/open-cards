# Taylor Series

## 🧠 Big Idea

A Taylor series is an infinite polynomial built to represent a function near a chosen center. It takes the idea of a Taylor polynomial and lets the degree go on forever.

Instead of approximating a function with only a few terms, a Taylor series uses all derivative information at one point. When it converges to the function, it gives a powerful way to replace a complicated function with an infinite sum of simple powers.

This is foundational because many functions in calculus, physics, and engineering are studied through their Taylor series. The series can reveal local behavior, make approximations easier, and help compute values that are hard to get directly.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n
$$

This says that $f(x)$ may be represented by an infinite sum centered at $a$. The symbol $f^{(n)}(a)$ means the $n$th derivative of $f$ evaluated at $a$, $n!$ is the factorial scaling factor, and $(x-a)^n$ measures the $n$th power of the distance from the center.

The first few terms are

$$
f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2+\cdots
$$

These terms match the function's value, slope, curvature, and higher-order behavior at $a$.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine trying to describe a curve by standing at one point on it. First you record the height. Then you record the slope. Then how the slope is changing. Then higher patterns of bending. A Taylor series packages all of that local information into one infinite polynomial.

Close to the center, the early terms often give excellent approximations. Farther away, convergence must be checked; an infinite formula is only useful where it actually converges to the function.

## 🔍 Simple Example

For $f(x)=e^x$ centered at $0$, every derivative is $e^x$, and each derivative value at $0$ is $1$. Therefore

$$
e^x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}
$$

This means $e^x$ is represented by $1+x+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots$ for every real $x$.

## ⚠️ Careful

A Taylor series is not automatically equal to the original function everywhere. You must know where the series converges and whether its sum is actually $f(x)$.

## 🔑 Remember

A Taylor series turns derivative information at one point into an infinite polynomial representation.
