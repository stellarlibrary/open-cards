# Area Under a Curve

## 🧠 Big Idea

When a function is nonnegative, the definite integral gives the area between the graph and the $x$-axis.

This idea turns curved shapes into something measurable. Instead of needing a special geometry formula for every curve, calculus cuts the region into thin slices and adds their limiting total.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\text{Area}=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \quad \text{when } f(x)\ge 0
$$

This means the area under $f$ from $x=a$ to $x=b$ equals the definite integral, provided the graph stays on or above the $x$-axis. The limits $a$ and $b$ mark the left and right edges of the region.

The condition $f(x)\ge 0$ matters because ordinary area is never negative.

## 👀 Intuition

Picture the region under a curve as a flexible sheet. Riemann sums approximate the sheet using rectangles. As the rectangles get thinner, their total area settles toward the true area.

The integral is not guessing the area; it defines the exact value through this limiting process.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Find the area under $f(x)=x$ from $0$ to $3$. The region is a right triangle with base $3$ and height $3$, so its area is $\frac{1}{2}\cdot 3\cdot 3=\frac{9}{2}$.

The integral agrees: $\int_0^3 x\,dx=\frac{9}{2}$.

## ⚠️ Careful

“Area under the curve” usually assumes the function is above the axis. If the graph goes below, the definite integral gives net area instead.

## 🔑 Remember

For nonnegative functions, the definite integral is the exact area under the graph.
