# Absolute Convergence

## 🧠 Big Idea

A series converges absolutely when it still converges after every term is made nonnegative. This is a stronger kind of convergence because it does not rely on cancellation between positive and negative terms.

If the total amount of size is finite, then the signed total is also stable.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |a_n| \text{ converges } \Longrightarrow \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \text{ converges}
$$

This means absolute convergence of the positive size series guarantees convergence of the original signed series. The absolute values measure total magnitude, ignoring signs.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine deposits and withdrawals in an account. Conditional cancellation may hide large back-and-forth movement, but absolute convergence says the total movement itself is finite.

That makes the final balance robust: rearranging absolutely convergent terms does not change the sum.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Consider $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^n\frac{1}{n^2}$. Its absolute value series is

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|(-1)^n\frac{1}{n^2}\right|=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}
$$

This means ignoring the signs gives a convergent $p$-series with $p=2$. Therefore the original alternating series converges absolutely.

## 🔗 Connection

Ratio and root tests usually prove absolute convergence because they use $|a_n|$.

## ⚠️ Careful

Absolute convergence is stronger than ordinary convergence. A series can converge without converging absolutely, but not the other way around.

## 🔑 Remember

Absolute convergence means the series converges even after all signs are ignored.
