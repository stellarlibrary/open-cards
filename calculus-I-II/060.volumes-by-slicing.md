# Volumes by Slicing

## 🧠 Big Idea

Volumes by slicing turns a three-dimensional volume problem into a one-dimensional integral.

If you know the area of each cross-section of a solid, then the total volume comes from adding those cross-sectional areas across the solid. Each slice is very thin, but together the slices fill the whole shape.

The method is powerful because the cross-sections do not have to be circles; they can be squares, triangles, semicircles, or other shapes.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
V=\int_a^b A(x)\,dx
$$

Here $V$ is volume, $[a,b]$ is the interval over which the solid extends, and $A(x)$ is the area of the cross-section perpendicular to the $x$-axis at position $x$. The small factor $dx$ represents a thin slice thickness.

## 👀 Intuition

Picture a loaf of bread. Each slice has a face area and a small thickness. If you added the volumes of all slices, you would recover the volume of the loaf.

Calculus lets the slices become infinitely thin, so the approximation becomes exact.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Suppose a solid runs from $x=0$ to $x=2$, and each cross-section perpendicular to the $x$-axis is a square with side length $x$.

Then $A(x)=x^2$, so the volume is $\int_0^2 x^2\,dx=\frac{8}{3}$. The square area changes as $x$ changes, and the integral accumulates those changing slice areas.

## ⚠️ Careful

The hardest part is often finding $A(x)$ correctly from the geometry of the slice.

## 🔑 Remember

Volume by slicing means add up cross-sectional areas.
