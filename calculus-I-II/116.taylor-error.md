# Taylor's Remainder and Error

## 🧠 Big Idea

Taylor's remainder measures how far a Taylor polynomial is from the actual function. A Taylor polynomial gives an approximation; the remainder tells the size of the missing tail.

This matters because approximations are only useful when we can control their error. Taylor's theorem gives a way to say, “The true value equals the polynomial plus a leftover term.”

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
f(x)=P_n(x)+R_n(x)
$$

This means the function value equals the degree $n$ Taylor polynomial plus a remainder term. The polynomial $P_n(x)$ is the part we compute, and $R_n(x)$ is the error from stopping after degree $n$.

One common form is Lagrange's remainder:

$$
R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}
$$

Here $c$ is some number between $a$ and $x$. This formula shows that the error depends on the next derivative, the distance from the center, and the factorial in the denominator.

## 👀 Intuition

Stopping a Taylor series is like zooming in with only a limited number of detail layers. The polynomial captures the first layers of behavior near the center. The remainder is everything still not captured.

If the next derivative is not too large and $x$ is close to $a$, the error is usually small.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Approximate $e^x$ near $0$ using

$$
P_2(x)=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2}
$$

This polynomial uses terms through degree $2$. For $x=0.1$, the next-size error is controlled by

$$
|R_2(0.1)|=\frac{e^c}{3!}(0.1)^3
$$

where $c$ lies between $0$ and $0.1$. Since $e^c<e^{0.1}$, the error is less than $\frac{e^{0.1}}{6}(0.001)$, which is very small.

## 🔑 Remember

Taylor's remainder is the controlled leftover after stopping a Taylor polynomial.
