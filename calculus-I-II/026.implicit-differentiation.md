# Implicit Differentiation

## 🧠 Big Idea

Sometimes a curve is defined by an equation that mixes $x$ and $y$ together, with no clean "$y=$" form. Implicit differentiation lets you find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ without solving for $y$ first.

The trick: treat $y$ as a function of $x$ and apply the chain rule whenever you differentiate a $y$ term.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, remembering:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\big[y^n\big]=n y^{n-1}\frac{dy}{dx}
$$

This means: every time you differentiate a $y$ term, you must tack on a $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factor, because $y$ secretly depends on $x$. Then solve the resulting equation for $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

## 👀 Intuition

Even when $y$ isn't isolated, it still changes as $x$ changes. The chain rule's extra $\frac{dy}{dx}$ records that hidden dependence, so you can capture the slope of curves like circles that aren't functions.

## 🔍 Simple Example

For the circle $x^2+y^2=25$, differentiate both sides:

$$
2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{x}{y}
$$

## ⚠️ Careful

Don't forget the $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factor on $y$ terms — that omission is the most common mistake. Constants and pure $x$ terms differentiate normally.

## 🔗 Connection

Implicit differentiation is really just the **chain rule** applied to $y$, since $y$ is a hidden function of $x$. It's also the engine behind **related rates**, where the hidden variable is time instead.

## 🔑 Remember

Differentiate both sides, attach $\frac{dy}{dx}$ to every $y$ term, then solve for it.
