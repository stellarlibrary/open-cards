# Integrating Power Series

## 🧠 Big Idea

A power series can be integrated term by term inside its interval of convergence. This lets us build antiderivatives and new function formulas from series we already know.

Just like differentiating, integrating a power series works because inside the radius of convergence the infinite polynomial behaves nicely enough for ordinary calculus rules to apply.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\int \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x-a)^n\,dx
=
C+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{c_n}{n+1}(x-a)^{n+1}
$$

This means each term is integrated using the power rule. The exponent increases from $n$ to $n+1$, the coefficient is divided by $n+1$, and a constant of integration $C$ is added.

The integrated series has the same radius of convergence as the original series, but endpoints must still be checked separately.

## 👀 Intuition

Integrating a power series is like accumulating the area under an infinite polynomial. Each power contributes its own small area formula, and the total antiderivative is built by adding those contributions.

This method is especially helpful when a function has no elementary antiderivative.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Start with

$$
\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n \quad \text{for } |x|<1
$$

This says the geometric series represents $\frac{1}{1-x}$ on $(-1,1)$.

Integrate both sides from $0$ to $x$:

$$
-\ln(1-x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}
$$

So the series becomes $x+\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}+\cdots$, a power series for $-\ln(1-x)$.

## ⚠️ Careful

Integration may change whether endpoints converge, even though the radius stays the same.

## 🔑 Remember

Inside its radius, a power series integrates term by term like a polynomial.
