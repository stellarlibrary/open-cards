# Motion Problems

## 🧠 Big Idea

Motion problems use integrals to connect velocity, position, and accumulated movement.

Velocity tells how position is changing at each instant. Integrating velocity over time adds up all those tiny changes in position, giving displacement. If velocity is sometimes negative, displacement keeps track of direction.

This makes motion a natural application of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
s(b)-s(a)=\int_a^b v(t)\,dt
$$

Here $s(t)$ is position at time $t$, $v(t)$ is velocity, and $[a,b]$ is the time interval. The left side $s(b)-s(a)$ is displacement: final position minus initial position. The integral adds the signed changes caused by velocity.

## 👀 Intuition

Think of velocity as a moment-by-moment instruction: move forward fast, move forward slowly, or move backward.

The integral combines all those instructions into one net change in position. Positive velocity increases position, while negative velocity decreases it.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Suppose a particle has velocity $v(t)=2t+1$ meters per second from $t=0$ to $t=3$.

Its displacement is $\int_0^3 (2t+1)\,dt=12$ meters. This means the particle ends $12$ meters farther along the line than where it started.

## ⚠️ Careful

Displacement is not always total distance traveled. Total distance uses $\int_a^b |v(t)|\,dt$, because distance ignores direction. A round trip can have zero displacement but a large total distance.

## 🔗 Connection

The same idea stacks one level up. Just as integrating velocity recovers position, integrating **acceleration** recovers velocity:

$$
v(b)-v(a)=\int_a^b a(t)\,dt
$$

This means the change in velocity is the accumulated effect of acceleration over the time interval.

## 🔑 Remember

Integrating velocity gives displacement, while integrating speed gives total distance.
