# The Derivative Definition

## 🧠 Big Idea

The formal definition of the derivative builds the instantaneous rate of change out of a **limit of average rates**. It's where the derivative officially comes from.

Every shortcut rule you'll learn — power rule, product rule, chain rule — is ultimately proven from this one definition.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
$$

This means:

- $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ is the slope of the **secant** line over a step of size $h$
- taking $h \to 0$ shrinks the step until the secant becomes the **tangent**

The result is the slope of the curve at the exact point $x$.

## 👀 Intuition

Think of zooming into a smooth curve. The more you zoom, the straighter it looks. The derivative is the slope of that "infinitely zoomed-in" line — the average slope over a vanishingly small step.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Find the derivative of $f(x)=x^2$ from the definition:

$$
\frac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{x^2+2xh+h^2-x^2}{h}=\frac{2xh+h^2}{h}=2x+h
$$

Now let $h \to 0$:

$$
f'(x)=\lim_{h \to 0}(2x+h)=2x
$$

## ⚠️ Careful

You can't just plug in $h=0$ at the start — that gives $\frac{0}{0}$. You must simplify the difference quotient *first*, then take the limit. The two notations $f'(x)$ and $\frac{dy}{dx}$ both name this same limit.

## 🔑 Remember

The derivative is a limit of secant slopes: simplify the difference quotient, then send $h \to 0$.
