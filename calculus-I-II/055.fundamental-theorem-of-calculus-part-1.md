# FTC Part 1: The Derivative of an Integral

## 🧠 Big Idea

FTC Part 1 says that accumulation and differentiation undo each other. If you build a new function by accumulating area under $f$ from a fixed starting point to a moving endpoint $x$, then the derivative of that accumulation function is the original function value $f(x)$.

In plain language: the rate at which accumulated area is growing equals the current height of the graph.

This is one of the deepest links in calculus. A definite integral measures total accumulated change, while a derivative measures instantaneous change. FTC Part 1 says those two ideas are not separate; they are inverse viewpoints on the same process.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=f(x)
$$

This means we first define an accumulation function by integrating $f(t)$ from the fixed number $a$ to the moving endpoint $x$, then differentiate that accumulated amount with respect to $x$. The variable $t$ is a dummy integration variable, while $x$ controls the upper limit. If $f$ is continuous, the derivative is exactly $f(x)$.

Equivalently, if

$$
A(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt
$$

then $A(x)$ is accumulated area from $a$ to $x$, and its derivative satisfies $A'(x)=f(x)$.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine filling a tank at a changing rate $f(t)$. The function $A(x)$ tells how much water has entered by time $x$. If you ask how fast $A(x)$ is increasing right now, the answer is the current inflow rate $f(x)$.

For area, moving the endpoint slightly to the right adds a very thin strip. The strip’s width is tiny, and its height is approximately $f(x)$. So the instant rate of area growth is the height of the curve.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Let $A(x)=\int_0^x t^2\,dt$. FTC Part 1 says $A'(x)=x^2$.

This means the derivative of the accumulated area under $t^2$ from $0$ to $x$ is the current height of the graph, $x^2$.

## Why It Matters

FTC Part 1 explains why integrals and derivatives belong together. It lets us create antiderivatives from accumulation functions and gives the conceptual foundation for evaluating many definite integrals.

## ⚠️ Careful

The formula works directly when the upper limit is $x$. If the upper limit is something like $x^2$, the chain rule is also needed.

## 🔑 Remember

The derivative of accumulated area is the current height of the function.
