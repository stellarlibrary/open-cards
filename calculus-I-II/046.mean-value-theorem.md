# Mean Value Theorem

## 🧠 Big Idea

The Mean Value Theorem says that if a function is smooth enough on an interval, then at some point its instantaneous rate of change equals its average rate of change over the whole interval.

In other words, somewhere on the trip, your speedometer must match your average speed.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, then there is some $c$ in $(a,b)$ such that

$$
f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}
$$

The left side, $f'(c)$, is the instantaneous slope at one point. The right side is the slope of the secant line through $(a,f(a))$ and $(b,f(b))$. The theorem says these slopes must match somewhere inside the interval.

## Why It Matters

The Mean Value Theorem connects local behavior to overall behavior. It explains why derivative information can control a whole function, not just one point.

## 👀 Intuition

Suppose you drive $120$ miles in $2$ hours. Your average speed is $60$ mph. If your motion is continuous and your speed changes smoothly, then at some instant you must have been going exactly $60$ mph.

The graph version says a tangent line is parallel to the secant line somewhere inside the interval.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Let $f(x)=x^2$ on $[1,3]$. The average slope is

$$
\frac{f(3)-f(1)}{3-1}=\frac{9-1}{2}=4
$$

Since $f'(x)=2x$, we solve $2c=4$ and get $c=2$. At $x=2$, the tangent slope equals the average slope on $[1,3]$.

## ⚠️ Careful

Continuity on $[a,b]$ and differentiability on $(a,b)$ are required. Corners, jumps, or breaks can make the conclusion fail.

## 🔑 Remember

The Mean Value Theorem guarantees one tangent slope equals the average slope.
