# Derivatives of Logarithmic Functions

## 🧠 Big Idea

The natural logarithm has a surprisingly simple derivative: $1/x$. A curve that grows ever more slowly has a slope that shrinks as $x$ increases.

Because $\ln x$ is the inverse of $e^x$, its derivative falls out naturally from the exponential rule.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac{1}{x}
$$

This means: the slope of $\ln x$ at input $x$ is exactly $1/x$. The bigger $x$ gets, the flatter the curve becomes. For a general base $a$:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\log_a x=\frac{1}{x \ln a}
$$

The factor $\ln a$ again shows why base $e$ is the simplest choice.

## 👀 Intuition

The log undoes the exponential. Since $e^x$ shoots up steeply, its inverse $\ln x$ must rise gently — and ever more gently — so its slope $1/x$ decreases as $x$ grows.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Differentiate $h(x)=\ln(x^2+1)$ with the chain rule:

$$
h'(x)=\frac{1}{x^2+1}\cdot 2x=\frac{2x}{x^2+1}
$$

## ⚠️ Careful

$\ln x$ is only defined for $x>0$, so its derivative $1/x$ applies there. For $\ln|x|$, the derivative is still $1/x$, now valid for all $x \neq 0$.

## 🔗 Connection

This rule powers **logarithmic differentiation**: for messy products, quotients, or powers like $x^x$, take $\ln$ of both sides first. The log turns multiplication into addition, making the derivative far easier.

## 🔑 Remember

The derivative of $\ln x$ is $1/x$ — the log's slope fades as $x$ grows.
