# Riemann Sums

## 🧠 Big Idea

A Riemann sum estimates accumulated quantity by cutting an interval into small pieces and adding up simple rectangle contributions.

Each rectangle has width $\Delta x$ and height chosen from the function. The more, thinner rectangles we use, the better the sum can describe the total accumulation.

Riemann sums are the bridge from ordinary area formulas to definite integrals.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x
$$

This means we add $n$ rectangle areas. The symbol $\sum$ means “add them up,” $\Delta x$ is the width of each subinterval, and $f(x_i^*)$ is the rectangle height chosen from the $i$th subinterval.

For equal-width pieces on $[a,b]$,

$$
\Delta x=\frac{b-a}{n}
$$

Here $b-a$ is the total interval length, and dividing by $n$ gives the width of each equal slice.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine estimating the area under a curved roof by placing thin rectangular boards underneath it. Wide boards give a rough outline. Narrow boards follow the curve more closely.

The sum may overestimate or underestimate depending on where the sample points are chosen.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Estimate the area under $f(x)=x$ on $[0,2]$ using two right-endpoint rectangles. Then $\Delta x=1$, and the heights are $f(1)=1$ and $f(2)=2$.

The Riemann sum is $1\cdot 1+2\cdot 1=3$, an estimate of the accumulated area.

## 🔗 Connection

A definite integral is what a Riemann sum approaches as the rectangles become infinitely thin.

## 🔑 Remember

A Riemann sum adds many small rectangle contributions to estimate total accumulation.
