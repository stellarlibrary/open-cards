# The Definite Integral

## 🧠 Big Idea

A definite integral measures exact net accumulation over an interval. It is built from Riemann sums, but instead of using a fixed number of rectangles, it captures the limiting value as rectangles become infinitely thin.

Unlike an indefinite integral, a definite integral produces a number, not a family of functions.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx
$$

This means the net accumulation of $f(x)$ from $x=a$ to $x=b$. The lower limit $a$ is where accumulation starts, the upper limit $b$ is where it ends, $f(x)$ gives the changing rate or height, and $dx$ indicates tiny changes in $x$.

As a limit of Riemann sums,

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i^*)\,\Delta x
$$

This says the integral equals the limiting value of rectangle sums as the number of rectangles grows without bound and their widths shrink toward $0$.

## 👀 Intuition

Think of a faucet with a changing flow rate. The definite integral tells how much water accumulates during a time interval.

If the function is above the $x$-axis, the integral adds positive area. If it is below, it adds negative area.

## 🔍 Simple Example

For $f(x)=2$ on $[1,4]$, the height is constantly $2$ and the width is $3$.

So $\int_1^4 2\,dx=6$, meaning the net accumulation is the area of a rectangle with height $2$ and width $3$.

## ⚠️ Careful

A definite integral is signed. Parts below the $x$-axis subtract from the total.

## 🔑 Remember

A definite integral is exact net accumulation over an interval.
