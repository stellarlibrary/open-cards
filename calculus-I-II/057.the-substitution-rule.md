# The Substitution Rule

## 🧠 Big Idea

The substitution rule is the integration version of the chain rule. It helps undo a composition when part of the integrand looks like the derivative of an inside function.

The main goal is to rename the inside expression so the integral becomes simpler. After integrating in the new variable, we change back to the original variable unless the limits have also been changed.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du, \quad u=g(x)
$$

This means that when $u$ is the inside function $g(x)$, the differential $du$ corresponds to $g'(x)\,dx$. The complicated expression $f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx$ becomes the simpler expression $f(u)\,du$.

For definite integrals, the limits can change too:

$$
\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du
$$

This means the new lower and upper limits are the old endpoint values passed through $g$.

## 👀 Intuition

Substitution is like changing the measuring scale. If the expression is naturally built around $3x+1$, it may be easier to measure everything using $u=3x+1$ instead of $x$.

Good substitutions reveal a hidden simpler integral.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Find $\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx$. Let $u=x^2$, so $du=2x\,dx$.

Then the integral becomes $\int \cos(u)\,du=\sin(u)+C$. Changing back gives $\sin(x^2)+C$.

## ⚠️ Careful

Substitution must account for the derivative of the inside function. If the matching factor is missing, the substitution may need adjustment or may not work.

## 🔑 Remember

Substitution works when an inside function and its derivative appear together.
