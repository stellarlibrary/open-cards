# Polar Area

## 🧠 Big Idea

Polar area adds up tiny wedge-shaped pieces instead of vertical rectangles.

In polar coordinates, a curve is built by rotating through angles. So the natural area slice is a thin sector with radius $r$ and tiny angle width $d\theta$. That is why the formula uses $r^2$, not just $r$.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
A=\int_\alpha^\beta \frac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta
$$

This means we add the areas of many tiny sectors from angle $\alpha$ to angle $\beta$. The radius $r$ is usually a function of $\theta$, so the distance from the origin can change as the angle rotates.

The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ comes from the area formula for a sector of a circle.

## 👀 Intuition

For ordinary area under a curve, we stack thin rectangles. For polar area, imagine slicing a pizza into very thin wedges.

Each wedge reaches from the origin out to the curve. Longer radii make much larger wedges, which explains why squaring $r$ matters.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Find the area inside the polar curve

$$
r=2, \qquad 0\le \theta\le 2\pi
$$

This means the curve is a circle of radius $2$ centered at the origin. The polar area is

$$
A=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}(2)^2\,d\theta=4\pi
$$

This matches the familiar circle area $\pi r^2=4\pi$.

## ⚠️ Careful

Choose angle limits that trace the region exactly once. Tracing a loop twice will double the area.

## 🔑 Remember

Polar area is built from tiny sectors, so the formula uses $\frac{1}{2}r^2$.
