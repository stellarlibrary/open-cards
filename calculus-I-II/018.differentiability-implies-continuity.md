# Differentiability Implies Continuity

## 🧠 Big Idea

If a function has a derivative at a point, it must be continuous there. Smoothness requires unbrokenness — you can't have a well-defined slope at a gap or jump.

But the reverse is **not** true: a function can be continuous yet still fail to have a derivative.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
f \text{ differentiable at } a \;\Longrightarrow\; f \text{ continuous at } a
$$

This means: differentiability is the *stronger* condition. Having a tangent slope guarantees the function is connected there, but being connected does not guarantee a tangent slope.

The arrow only points one way.

## 👀 Intuition

To have a single slope at a point, the curve must be smooth there — no breaks and no sharp corners. A break has no slope because the function jumps. A corner has no slope because the left and right sides disagree on direction.

## 🔍 Simple Example

The function $f(x)=|x|$ is continuous everywhere, including at $x=0$.

But at $x=0$ it has a sharp corner: the slope is $-1$ from the left and $+1$ from the right. These disagree, so $f'(0)$ does not exist.

This shows continuity alone is not enough for differentiability.

## ⚠️ Careful

Don't reverse the rule. Continuous does **not** imply differentiable — corners, cusps, and vertical tangents are continuous but not differentiable.

## 🔑 Remember

Differentiable means continuous, but continuous doesn't mean differentiable — corners break the rule.
