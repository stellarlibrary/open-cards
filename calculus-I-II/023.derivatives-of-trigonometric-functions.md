# Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

## 🧠 Big Idea

The trig derivatives follow a clean, memorable pattern. The two you must know are sine and cosine; everything else builds from them.

These come from the limit definition together with the special limit $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1$.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x
$$

This means: differentiating sine gives cosine, and differentiating cosine gives **negative** sine. The minus sign on cosine is the detail students most often forget. From these,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2 x
$$

## 👀 Intuition

Sine and cosine describe circular motion. Where sine is climbing fastest (at $x=0$), its rate equals cosine's peak value of $1$. Where sine is at its top, it's momentarily flat, and indeed cosine is $0$ there. The derivative tracks the slope of the wave.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Differentiate $h(x)=\sin(x^2)$ using the chain rule:

$$
h'(x)=\cos(x^2)\cdot 2x=2x\cos(x^2)
$$

## ⚠️ Careful

These formulas assume $x$ is in **radians**. In degrees, extra constants appear, so always work in radians for calculus.

## 🔗 Connection

The other three follow from the quotient rule: $\frac{d}{dx}\sec x=\sec x\tan x$, $\frac{d}{dx}\csc x=-\csc x\cot x$, and $\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=-\csc^2 x$. Notice every "co-" function picks up a minus sign.

## 🔑 Remember

Sine goes to cosine; cosine goes to *negative* sine — don't drop the minus.
