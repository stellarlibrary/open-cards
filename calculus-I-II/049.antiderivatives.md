# Antiderivatives

## 🧠 Big Idea

An antiderivative reverses differentiation. Instead of asking, “What is the rate of change?” we ask, “What original function could have produced this derivative?”

If $F'(x)=f(x)$, then $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$. There is usually not just one answer, because adding a constant changes the height of a graph but not its slope.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
F'(x)=f(x)
$$

This means $F$ is a function whose derivative is $f$. The symbol $F'(x)$ is the rate of change of $F$, and $f(x)$ is the derivative we are trying to reverse.

All antiderivatives of $f$ have the form

$$
F(x)+C
$$

Here $C$ is any constant. It appears because functions that differ only by a vertical shift have the same derivative.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine seeing only the speedometer of a car. An antiderivative helps reconstruct a possible position function from that speed. But without knowing the starting position, many shifted position graphs are possible.

Graphically, antiderivatives are a family of curves with the same slope pattern everywhere.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Since the derivative of $x^3$ is $3x^2$, an antiderivative of $3x^2$ is $x^3$.

In fact, every function $x^3+C$ has derivative $3x^2$, because the derivative of the constant $C$ is $0$.

## ⚠️ Careful

An antiderivative is not unique. Forgetting the possible constant shifts loses most of the family.

## 🔑 Remember

Antiderivatives are functions whose derivatives give you the function you started with.
