# FTC Part 2: Evaluating Definite Integrals

## 🧠 Big Idea

FTC Part 2 gives the practical way to evaluate many definite integrals. Instead of adding infinitely many rectangles directly, we find an antiderivative and subtract its values at the endpoints.

This theorem says that total accumulated change over an interval equals final amount minus initial amount. If $F'(x)=f(x)$, then $F$ is a function whose rate of change is $f$. Accumulating that rate from $a$ to $b$ gives the net change in $F$.

So the definite integral is not just area; it is also a change-measuring machine. It translates a continuous accumulation problem into an endpoint comparison, which is usually much easier to compute and easier to interpret.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)
$$

This means that if $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ on $[a,b]$, then the definite integral of $f$ from $a$ to $b$ equals the value of $F$ at the right endpoint minus the value of $F$ at the left endpoint. The $dx$ tells us the accumulation is with respect to $x$.

The condition $F'(x)=f(x)$ is essential. We may use $F$ only because differentiating $F$ gives back the integrand $f$. Continuity of $f$ on the interval is the usual setting that guarantees this connection behaves well.

## 👀 Intuition

Think about a position function $F(t)$ and velocity $f(t)$. If velocity is the rate of change of position, then integrating velocity over time gives net displacement.

You do not need to track every tiny movement separately if you know the starting and ending positions. The accumulated change is simply “where you ended” minus “where you started.”

## 🔍 Simple Example

Evaluate $\int_1^3 2x\,dx$. An antiderivative of $2x$ is $F(x)=x^2$.

Using FTC Part 2, $\int_1^3 2x\,dx=F(3)-F(1)=9-1=8$.

This means the net accumulation of $2x$ from $1$ to $3$ is $8$.

## Why It Matters

FTC Part 2 turns definite integrals from limiting sums into computable quantities. It is the reason antiderivatives are so powerful in area, motion, average value, and accumulated-change problems.

## ⚠️ Careful

Do not add $+C$ when evaluating $F(b)-F(a)$. The constant would cancel, so any one antiderivative works.

## 🔑 Remember

A definite integral equals antiderivative at the end minus antiderivative at the start.
