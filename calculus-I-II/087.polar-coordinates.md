# Polar Coordinates

## 🧠 Big Idea

Polar coordinates locate a point by distance and direction instead of horizontal and vertical position.

A point $(r,\theta)$ says: start at the origin, turn to angle $\theta$, then move distance $r$ along that direction. This is often natural for circles, spirals, and curves built around a center.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
x=r\cos\theta, \qquad y=r\sin\theta
$$

This means the usual Cartesian coordinates can be found from the polar distance $r$ and angle $\theta$. The factor $\cos\theta$ gives the horizontal part of the distance, and $\sin\theta$ gives the vertical part.

The number $r$ measures distance from the pole, which is the origin. The angle $\theta$ measures direction from the positive $x$-axis.

## 👀 Intuition

Cartesian coordinates sound like walking along a grid: move right, then move up. Polar coordinates sound like using a compass: face a direction, then walk forward.

This makes polar coordinates powerful when the geometry is circular. Instead of forcing round shapes onto a rectangular grid, we describe them from the center outward.

## 🔍 Simple Example

The polar point

$$
(2,\pi/3)
$$

This means the point is $2$ units from the origin at an angle of $\pi/3$. Converting gives

$$
x=2\cos(\pi/3)=1, \qquad y=2\sin(\pi/3)=\sqrt{3}
$$

So the same point in Cartesian coordinates is $(1,\sqrt{3})$.

## ⚠️ Careful

Polar coordinates are not unique: adding $2\pi$ to the angle, or using a negative radius with the opposite direction, can describe the same point.

## 🔑 Remember

Polar coordinates say how far from the origin and in what direction.
