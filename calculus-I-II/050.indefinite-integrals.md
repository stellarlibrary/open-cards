# Indefinite Integrals

## 🧠 Big Idea

An indefinite integral is the notation we use for the whole family of antiderivatives. It does not give a number; it gives functions.

When you see an indefinite integral, think: “Find all functions whose derivative is this integrand.” The answer must include $+C$, because infinitely many vertical shifts have the same derivative.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C
$$

This means $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$, so $F'(x)=f(x)$. The symbol $\int$ signals integration, $f(x)$ is the function being integrated, $dx$ tells us the variable is $x$, and $C$ represents any constant.

The $dx$ is not decoration. It reminds us which variable is changing, which matters especially when more than one variable appears.

## 👀 Intuition

Differentiation breaks a function into its rate of change. Indefinite integration works backward, rebuilding possible original functions from that rate.

Picture a stack of parallel curves. They all have the same slope at each $x$-value, but they sit at different heights. The indefinite integral names the whole stack.

## 🔍 Simple Example

To integrate $4x^3$, ask what function has derivative $4x^3$. Since the derivative of $x^4$ is $4x^3$,

$$
\int 4x^3\,dx=x^4+C
$$

This says every function $x^4+C$ has derivative $4x^3$, no matter what constant $C$ is.

## ⚠️ Careful

Leaving off $+C$ changes the meaning. It gives only one antiderivative, not the entire indefinite integral.

## 🔑 Remember

An indefinite integral is a family of antiderivatives, so always include $+C$.
