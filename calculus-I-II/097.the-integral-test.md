# The Integral Test

## 🧠 Big Idea

The integral test connects infinite sums with improper integrals. When a series has positive, decreasing terms coming from a continuous function, the area under the curve predicts whether the sum is finite.

It does not usually give the exact sum. It tells whether the total converges or diverges.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a_n \text{ and } \int_1^{\infty} f(x)\,dx \text{ either both converge or both diverge}
$$

This applies when $a_n=f(n)$ and $f$ is positive, continuous, and decreasing for $x\ge 1$. The series adds rectangle heights, while the integral measures the related area.

## 👀 Intuition

Picture the terms of the series as heights of rectangles sitting along the $x$-axis. If the curve decreases smoothly, the rectangle total and the area under the curve control each other.

So if the area stretches to infinity, the sum does too. If the area is finite, the sum is also finite.

## 🔍 Simple Example

For $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}$, use $f(x)=\frac{1}{x^2}$, which is positive, continuous, and decreasing on $[1,\infty)$.

$$
\int_1^{\infty}\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx=1
$$

This means the improper integral has finite area. Therefore $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}$ converges by the integral test.

## ⚠️ Careful

The integral test requires the positivity, continuity, and decreasing conditions eventually. It compares convergence behavior, not the exact numerical sum.

## 🔑 Remember

The integral test turns a difficult infinite sum into an improper-integral convergence question.
