# Differentiating Power Series

## 🧠 Big Idea

A power series can be differentiated term by term inside its interval of convergence. This means you can treat it much like an infinite polynomial, as long as you stay within the radius of convergence.

This is one reason power series are so useful: they let us create new series from old ones and represent derivatives of functions using simpler polynomial pieces.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(x-a)^n
=
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n c_n(x-a)^{n-1}
$$

This means each term is differentiated using the power rule. The constant term $c_0$ disappears, and each remaining coefficient is multiplied by its power $n$.

The differentiated series has the same radius of convergence as the original series, although endpoint behavior may change.

## 👀 Intuition

Differentiating a power series is like finding the slope of an infinitely long polynomial. Near the center, the polynomial pieces fit together well enough that the usual derivative rules still work.

The result tells how the function represented by the original series is changing.

## 🔍 Simple Example

Start with the geometric series

$$
\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} x^n \quad \text{for } |x|<1
$$

This means the function $\frac{1}{1-x}$ is represented by the sum $1+x+x^2+\cdots$ on that interval.

Differentiate both sides:

$$
\frac{1}{(1-x)^2}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n x^{n-1}
$$

So differentiating the geometric series gives a new series for $\frac{1}{(1-x)^2}$, still valid for $|x|<1$.

## ⚠️ Careful

Term-by-term differentiation is guaranteed inside the interval, not necessarily at the endpoints.

## 🔑 Remember

Inside its radius, a power series differentiates like an infinite polynomial.
