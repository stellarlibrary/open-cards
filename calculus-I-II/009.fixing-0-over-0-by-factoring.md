# Fixing $0/0$ by Factoring

## 🧠 Big Idea

When a rational limit gives $\frac{0}{0}$, factoring often saves the day. A shared factor on the top and bottom is what creates the hole, and canceling it reveals the real behavior.

This is the go-to method when both pieces are polynomials.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\lim_{x \to a} \frac{(x-a)\,p(x)}{(x-a)\,q(x)}=\lim_{x \to a}\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}
$$

This means: if both top and bottom contain the factor $(x-a)$, that common factor is the cause of the $\frac{0}{0}$. Canceling it leaves a simpler expression you can evaluate by substitution.

## 👀 Intuition

The factor $(x-a)$ is the "troublemaker" that makes both top and bottom zero. Since the limit only cares about $x$ **near** $a$ (not at $a$), it's legal to cancel that factor and look at what remains.

## 🔍 Simple Example

$$
\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2-4}{x-2}
$$

Substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$. Factor the top:

$$
\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2
$$

Now substitute:

$$
\lim_{x \to 2}(x+2)=4
$$

## ⚠️ Careful

Canceling changes the function at the single point $x=a$, but that's fine — the limit ignores that exact point. The simplified function gives the correct limit.

## 🔗 Connection

The factor you cancel is what creates a **removable discontinuity** (a hole) in the original graph. The limit value you find is exactly the height that would "plug" that hole.

## 🔑 Remember

Factor, cancel the common $(x-a)$, then substitute into what's left.
