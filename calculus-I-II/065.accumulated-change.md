# Accumulated Change

## 🧠 Big Idea

Accumulated change is the total amount a quantity gains or loses when its rate of change varies.

A rate tells what is happening at each instant. An integral adds those tiny changes over an interval, turning “how fast it changes” into “how much it changed.”

This idea appears in motion, population growth, water flow, temperature change, cost, and many other applications.

## 📐 Equation Meaning

$$
Q(b)-Q(a)=\int_a^b Q'(t)\,dt
$$

Here $Q(t)$ is the quantity being measured, $Q'(t)$ is its rate of change, and $[a,b]$ is the interval of time or input. The left side is total change in $Q$, while the integral accumulates the rate over the interval.

## 👀 Intuition

Imagine filling a tank with a faucet whose flow rate changes over time. At each moment, a tiny amount of water enters the tank.

The integral adds all those tiny additions. Even if the rate is not constant, the total accumulated change is still the combined effect of the rate across time.

## 🔍 Simple Example

A tank fills at rate $r(t)=2+0.5t$ gallons per minute for $0\le t\le 4$.

The amount added is $\int_0^4 (2+0.5t)\,dt=12$ gallons. The rate changes with time, but the integral gives the total water added during the four minutes.

## 🔗 Connection

Accumulated change is the practical meaning of the definite integral and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

## 🔑 Remember

Integrating a rate gives the total change it produces.
